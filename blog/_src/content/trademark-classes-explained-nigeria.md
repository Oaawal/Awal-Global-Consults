A lot of business owners register a trademark, breathe a sigh of relief, and assume their brand is now protected — full stop. It isn't quite that simple. Trademark protection in Nigeria only extends to the specific "class" of goods or services you register it under. Pick the wrong class, and you can end up with a certificate that doesn't actually cover what your business does.

Here's what trademark classes are, how the system works, and how to choose correctly.

## What is a trademark class?

Nigeria uses the **Nice Classification system** — an internationally standardised list of 45 classes used to categorise every type of goods and service a trademark can cover. Classes 1 to 34 cover goods (physical products), and classes 35 to 45 cover services.

When you register a trademark, you're not registering the name or logo in the abstract — you're registering it *for use in connection with specific goods or services*, grouped into one or more of these classes. Your exclusive rights only apply within the class (or classes) you've filed under.

## Why this matters more than people expect

Two businesses can legally hold the same or a very similar trademark, as long as they operate in unrelated classes and there's no real risk of confusing customers. A furniture company called "Zenith" and a software company called "Zenith" can, in principle, both hold valid trademarks — they're not competing in the same space.

This cuts both ways for you:

- **If you register in the wrong class**, someone else can register an identical or similar mark in the class your business actually operates in — and you'd have no grounds to stop them, despite holding a valid certificate.
- **If you only register in one class but your business spans several** (say, you sell a physical product and also offer a related service), you're only protected for the one you filed under. A competitor could register your name for the other class.

## A few examples to make this concrete

- A clothing brand producing and selling apparel would typically look at **Class 25** (clothing, footwear, headgear) — and separately consider **Class 35** if they also want to protect their retail/e-commerce operation, since retail services sit in a different class from the goods themselves.
- A restaurant or food delivery business would generally consider **Class 43** (services for providing food and drink), while a packaged food producer would look at classes within the goods range (e.g. **Class 29** or **Class 30**, depending on the product).
- A software or fintech company usually falls under **Class 9** (software/technology products) and/or **Class 42** (technology and software design services), sometimes alongside **Class 36** if financial services are involved.
- A consulting or professional services firm — like a legal, compliance, or business advisory practice — typically falls under **Class 35** (business consulting, administration) or **Class 45** (legal and personal services), depending on exactly what's being offered.

These are starting points, not rules — the right class (or classes) depends on precisely how your business is described, and getting the description wrong within an otherwise correct class can also weaken your protection. This is the part of the process where professional input tends to pay for itself: it's a narrow, technical judgment call, and refiling because of a class error costs you both time and money.

## Can you register in more than one class?

Yes — and many established or multi-line businesses do. Each class filed is treated as a separate application for fee purposes, so registering across multiple classes costs more than a single-class filing, but it closes the gap that a narrow, single-class registration would leave open. The right approach is usually to register in every class that reflects what your business *actually does or plans to do soon* — not to speculatively file across dozens of unrelated classes "just in case."

## How class selection fits into the wider registration process

Choosing your class (or classes) happens early, right after your clearance search confirms the mark itself is available:

1. **Clearance search** — confirms nothing identical or confusingly similar is already registered or pending.
2. **Class selection** — identifying every class relevant to your actual goods/services.
3. **Filing** — application submitted to the Nigerian Industrial Property Office (NIPO), the body under the Federal Ministry of Industry, Trade and Investment responsible for trademarks (you'll also see it referred to by its older name, the Trademarks Registry).
4. **Examination** — NIPO reviews the application for conflicts and compliance.
5. **Publication and opposition window** — accepted marks are published in the Trademark Journal, giving existing rights-holders a window to object.
6. **Certificate issuance** — if unopposed (or opposition is resolved in your favour), your certificate is issued. Registration is valid for seven years and renewable indefinitely after that.

Keep in mind: a CAC business name or company registration does **not** give you trademark rights. It reserves your name in the commercial registry, but it doesn't stop a competitor from using a similar name or logo in trade — only a registered trademark does that. If you've already registered with the CAC, that's a separate (and equally necessary) step, not a substitute.
