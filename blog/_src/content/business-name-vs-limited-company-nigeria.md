Almost every first-time registrant asks the same question before anything else: business name, or limited company? It's usually framed as a cost question, business names are cheaper and faster, so the instinct is to default to whichever one is simpler right now. That instinct is right for some businesses and expensive for others, and the difference isn't about how big your business feels today. It's about a handful of specific factors that are easy to overlook until one of them bites.

## Start with the one that actually matters most: liability

A business name is not a separate legal person from you. Legally, you and the business are the same entity wearing two names. If the business can't pay a debt, defaults on a contract, or gets sued, that liability sits with you personally, your personal assets, not a corporate shield, are what's exposed.

A limited company is a genuinely separate legal person. It can own property, sign contracts, and owe debts in its own name. If it fails, what shareholders lose is generally limited to what they put in, not their personal assets beyond that (fraud and a handful of other exceptions aside).

::: callout
If your business involves meaningful contracts, credit, inventory, or anything where a dispute could realistically cost more than you'd want to lose personally, that alone is often reason enough to register a limited company, regardless of how "small" the business currently feels.
:::

## The questions worth actually answering

**Are you bringing in co-founders, investors, or shareholders?** A business name has no shareholding structure to speak of. If more than one person needs a defined, provable stake in the business, or if you're raising any kind of outside investment, a limited company is close to non-negotiable. Investors don't put money into a business name; there's no share to issue them.

**Who are you selling to?** Government tenders, corporate procurement, and larger B2B clients frequently require a limited company as a baseline eligibility condition, sometimes explicitly in the tender documentation. If that's the direction your business is heading, registering a business name now just means re-registering as a company later, at extra cost and with a gap in your paper trail in between.

**How much ongoing compliance can you realistically keep up with?** A business name's obligations are lighter: an annual return and not much else. A limited company carries more: statutory registers, annual returns, an AGM and auditor appointment within a set window after incorporation, and ongoing Persons with Significant Control (PSC) disclosure. None of it is difficult, but all of it needs to actually happen, consistently, not just at setup.

**Does your bank or payment processor expect it?** Some banking relationships and most serious payment integrations move more smoothly, or become possible at all, once you're dealing with a registered company rather than a sole proprietorship. If you're already running into friction opening a proper corporate account, that's a signal worth listening to.

## Where a business name is genuinely the right call

This isn't a case for always choosing the company. A business name is the correct, deliberate choice, not just the cheap fallback, when:

- You're a freelancer, consultant, or sole trader without co-founders or outside investment on the table
- Your client base is mostly individuals or small businesses rather than institutions with procurement requirements
- You want the lightest possible compliance load while you validate whether the business works at all
- You're not carrying meaningful contract or credit risk that would justify the liability separation

::: quote
The right structure is the one that matches what your business actually does and where it's actually going, not the one that felt simplest to click through on a form.
:::

## The comparison, side by side

| Factor | Business Name | Limited Company |
|---|---|---|
| Legal separation from owner | None — you are personally liable | Full — company is a separate legal person |
| Can raise investment / issue shares | No | Yes |
| Typical fit for government tenders / corporate clients | Often disqualifying | Usually required |
| Ongoing compliance | Minimal (annual return) | Higher (registers, AGM, PSC disclosure, annual returns) |
| Cost to register | Lower | Higher, and scales with share capital |
| Converting later | Requires a fresh company registration | N/A |

## You can convert later, but it's not free

Registering a business name doesn't lock you out of incorporating a company down the line, plenty of businesses do exactly that as they grow. But it's a separate registration with its own filing and cost, not an automatic upgrade, and there's a gap in your business's formal history while the conversion happens. If you can already see the company stage coming within the next year or so, there's a real argument for registering there directly rather than paying to move twice.
