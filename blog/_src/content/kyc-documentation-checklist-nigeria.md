Most businesses that need to run KYC on their clients start with a document checklist copied from somewhere else, then quietly find the gaps months later — usually when a bank, auditor, or regulator asks for something that was never collected. Here's what a genuinely complete KYC intake covers, broken down by client type, plus the parts that are easy to miss.

## Individual clients

- Full legal name, date of birth, nationality
- Valid government-issued ID — NIN, international passport, driver's licence, or voter's card. For higher-risk transactions, it's worth collecting two forms rather than one.
- BVN, where the relationship touches banking or financial services
- A recent passport photograph
- **Proof of address** — a utility bill, bank statement, or tenancy agreement, dated within the last three months. This is the piece most basic intake forms leave out entirely; a residential address typed into a form isn't the same as a document that verifies it.
- Phone number and email
- Occupation, and for higher-value engagements, source of funds

## Corporate clients

- CAC Certificate of Incorporation and current Status Report
- RC number and TIN (as of this year, your CAC RC number doubles as your TIN automatically)
- Memorandum & Articles of Association
- Directors' and shareholders' particulars — each backed by their own individual KYC (ID, address proof, the works)
- **Persons with Significant Control (PSC) disclosure** — name, percentage of ownership or control, nationality, and ID for anyone crossing the beneficial-ownership threshold (25% is the commonly used marker)
- Registered business address, with supporting proof
- A board resolution authorising whoever is instructing you on the company's behalf
- Nature of business and expected scope of engagement

## Incorporated Trustees / NGOs

- Certificate of Incorporation for Trustees
- The governing constitution or rules
- Each trustee's individual KYC

## The risk layer that sits on top of documents

Collecting documents is only half of KYC. The other half is what you do with them:

- **PEP screening** — is the client, or anyone with significant control, a Politically Exposed Person (currently or formerly holding a prominent public role, or closely connected to someone who does)?
- **Sanctions screening** — checking against relevant watch lists.
- **A risk rating** — low, medium, or high, based on client type, transaction size, and geography. This is the part regulators actually look for during an audit — not just "did you collect the documents," but "did you assess what they meant."
- **Ongoing review** — for relationships that continue over time, KYC isn't a one-time capture. Periodic re-verification matters, especially if something about the client's risk profile changes.

## Two structural things worth building in from day one

**Record retention.** Nigerian AML rules generally expect KYC records to be kept for a period after the relationship ends — five years is the figure most commonly cited. If your onboarding process deletes files once a case is marked complete, that's a gap worth closing before it becomes a problem during an audit.

**Data protection compliance on the collection itself.** You're gathering NIN, BVN, and government ID copies — exactly the category of data the Nigeria Data Protection Act treats seriously. A lawful basis for collecting it, a clearly stated purpose, secure storage, and a real consent step (not just a line buried in terms and conditions) all need to be part of the process, not an afterthought.

## If your business is a DNFBP, the bar is higher

If your firm is a Designated Non-Financial Business or Profession — law firms, accountants, real estate practices, and dealers in high-value goods often are — you're not just doing "onboarding," you're operating under SCUML/AML obligations with a stricter customer due diligence standard. That means source-of-funds questions and PEP declarations aren't optional extras; they're part of what SCUML expects you to be assessing on your own clients as a matter of course.

## Where this usually breaks down in practice

The gap almost never shows up at the individual-document level — most businesses remember to ask for an ID. It shows up in the structural pieces: proof of address as an actual document rather than a form field, PSC disclosure for corporate clients, a defined risk rating rather than a gut feeling, and a retention policy that doesn't quietly delete records the moment a file is marked "done." Those are the parts worth checking your current process against before an auditor does it for you.
