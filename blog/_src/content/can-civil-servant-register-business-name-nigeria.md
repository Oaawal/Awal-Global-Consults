It's one of the most common questions we get, and the honest answer is: **it depends on what "register a business" means for you.** Nigerian law doesn't stop public officers from earning anything outside their job. What it restricts is **managing or running** a private business while employed full time — with an important exception for farming.

::: callout
This guide explains the general rule. Your own position also depends on your terms of service and your employer's rules, so check with your HR or establishment department before you register, and keep a copy of any approval.
:::

## What the law actually says

The Code of Conduct for Public Officers, in the **Fifth Schedule to the 1999 Constitution**, says that a public officer shall not, **except where he is not employed on a full-time basis, engage or participate in the management or running of any private business, profession or trade.** It goes on to say that this does **not** prevent a public officer from **farming** or from managing or running a farming business.

Three things follow from that wording:

1. **It's about management, not ownership.** The restriction is on running or managing a business.
2. **Farming is expressly allowed.**
3. **It applies to full-time officers.** Part-time officers are outside this particular restriction.

The Code is enforced by the Code of Conduct Bureau and Tribunal, and breaches can also lead to disciplinary action under your service rules.

## Why a business name is the tricky case

A **business name** is registered in the names of its proprietors, and the proprietor is the person carrying on the business. If you register a business name in your own name and run it, that sits squarely within "management or running of a private business".

A **limited company** is different: the company is a separate legal person. Holding **shares** in a company, as an investor who isn't involved in running it, is generally treated differently from managing the business. Being a **director** who runs it day to day is not.

| Option | Generally fits the rule? |
|---|---|
| Registering a farming business (agriculture, poultry, fish farming and similar) | Yes — farming is expressly allowed |
| Holding shares in a company someone else runs | Generally yes, as a passive investor |
| Being a director or manager who runs a company day to day | No, while you're a full-time officer |
| Registering and running a business name in your own name | No, unless it's farming or you're not full time |
| Registering a business now, to start trading after you leave service | Discuss timing with your employer first |

## Safe ways to start

- **Start with farming.** If agriculture fits your plans, it is the clearest route, and a business name or company can be registered for it.
- **Invest, don't manage.** Take shares in a company run by someone else, with a clear understanding that you are not involved in management.
- **Plan for after service.** If you're close to retirement or resigning, prepare the structure and register at the right time.
- **Ask in writing.** Some services and agencies have their own rules or approval processes. A written answer protects you.

::: quote
Registering a business in a relative's name while you quietly run it doesn't remove the restriction — it just hides it. The structure should match who genuinely runs the business.
:::

## Declaring your assets

Public officers also declare their assets to the Code of Conduct Bureau. Shares and business interests are assets, so make sure what you own is reflected in your declarations.

## The short version

Full-time public officers shouldn't run a private business, but farming is allowed and passive ownership is treated differently from management. Choose a structure that matches who actually runs the business, confirm with your employer, and register with confidence.
