If you're running a business in Nigeria — a shop, an online store, a consultancy, or a growing company — registering with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) is the single most important step in making it official. It's what turns "a thing I do to make money" into a legal entity that can open a corporate bank account, sign contracts, bid for tenders, and build trust with customers and partners.

This guide walks through what CAC registration actually involves, the three main structures available to you, the process from start to finish, and what to expect once your certificate is issued.

## What is CAC registration?

The Corporate Affairs Commission is the federal body responsible for registering and regulating businesses in Nigeria under the Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA) 2020. Registering with the CAC gives your business (or company) a distinct legal identity, separate from you as an individual. Without it, you're operating informally — which limits what you can do and exposes you to more risk, not less.

## Choosing the right structure

Most people registering for the first time are choosing between three options:

**Business Name** is the simplest and most affordable route. It suits sole proprietors, freelancers, and small traders who want a registered name without the added compliance obligations of a company. A business name is not a separate legal entity from its owner — you remain personally liable for the business's debts and obligations.

**Private Limited Company (Ltd)** creates a separate legal entity from its owners. This is the standard structure for startups, growing SMEs, and any business planning to raise investment, take on multiple shareholders, or limit personal liability. It comes with more ongoing compliance obligations than a business name — annual returns, statutory registers, and (once you cross certain thresholds) audited accounts — but it's the structure most banks, investors, and larger corporate clients expect to see.

**Incorporated Trustees** is the structure for NGOs, foundations, religious bodies, and other not-for-profit associations. It has its own registration requirements, including published notice of intent to incorporate and a distinct governance structure (trustees rather than directors/shareholders).

If your business has foreign shareholders or investment, a Limited Company is required, and you'll also need to factor in NIPC (Nigerian Investment Promotion Commission) registration alongside your CAC filing — these two typically go hand in hand for foreign-owned entities.

## The registration process, step by step

1. **Name availability search and reservation.** You propose a name (usually with 1-2 alternatives), and the CAC checks it isn't already taken or too similar to an existing registered name. Once available, the name is reserved for you for a limited window while you complete the rest of the filing.
2. **Document preparation.** This differs by structure but generally includes valid ID for the proprietor/directors/trustees, passport photographs, proof of address, and (for companies) details of shareholders, share allocation, and registered office address.
3. **Online filing.** Registration is done entirely through the CAC's online portal — there's no requirement to visit a CAC office in person for standard registrations.
4. **Payment of statutory fees.** Fees differ by structure and, for companies, by share capital. Because these are set by CAC gazette and have changed more than once in recent years, it's worth confirming the current fee schedule directly rather than relying on a number you saw somewhere else — we can confirm the up-to-date figure for your specific structure when you reach out.
5. **Review and approval.** Once your documents are submitted and fees paid, the CAC reviews the filing. Business names are typically processed faster than companies; companies can take longer if documentation needs correction.
6. **Certificate issuance.** On approval, you receive your digital Certificate of Incorporation (for companies) or Certificate of Registration (for business names), carrying your unique CAC registration number.

## What changed recently: your CAC number now doubles as your tax ID

Under Nigeria's new tax administration framework that took effect from January 2026, a registered business's CAC registration number automatically serves as its Tax Identification Number (TIN), through CAC's integration with the tax authority. This removes a step that used to require separate registration with the tax authority after incorporation — your TIN now arrives with your CAC certificate rather than as a follow-up process.

Companies should also be aware of the **Persons with Significant Control (PSC)** requirement under CAMA 2020 — anyone who owns or controls a meaningful stake in the company must be disclosed to the CAC's beneficial ownership register, and any changes in control need to be reported within the statutory window. This is a compliance obligation that continues after incorporation, not just a one-time filing.

## After you're registered: what comes next

Getting your certificate is the start of your compliance obligations, not the end of them. Depending on your structure, you'll need to keep on top of:

- **Annual returns** — a yearly filing confirming your business or company is still active. Missing this consistently can lead to your entity being struck off the register.
- **Statutory registers** — for companies, registers of members, directors, and (where applicable) charges.
- **Appointment of auditors and your first AGM** — required for companies within specific windows after incorporation.
- **Updating the CAC** on any changes — registered address, directors, shareholding, or significant control.
- **Opening a corporate bank account**, which requires your certificate and registration number, and is usually the first practical thing businesses do once registered.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Choosing a business name that's too generic or too close to an existing registered name, causing rejection at the search stage.
- Registering as a business name when a limited company structure would actually serve the business better (especially if you're planning to raise funding or bring in partners).
- Treating the certificate as the finish line and missing the ongoing compliance calendar that follows.
- Using informal "agents" who promise unrealistic timelines or ask for payment outside the official channels.
